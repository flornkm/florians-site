## Designed to age

There was always something interesting in how some products we use every day look only better with time. Normally we'd assume that everything we buy, we buy because of how it looked initially. There are many examples for this, the iPhone that looks extremely well designed on Apple's website, the clean clothes models wear on fashion sites, or simply products we see when walking past them in showcases.

There are some types of products and their materials which - I believe - make them look only better with time. I want to use this occasion to explore these products to find out the _why_ behind this and what makes them look so much better when used. One suspicion upfront: wear works like evidence. It proves a product was part of someone's life, and some brands understood this so well that they even fake it.

### Rimowa

This is one of the best known examples: The Rimowa luggage. At the latest once you had to check it in for a flight you'll find scratches on it. After some time I realized how much I like this imperfect design - maybe because each detail represents a memory of a past experience.

### Aesop

Aesop does a great job using shots of their products in a _used_ state, rather than showing them perfectly. The pressed tubes look so much better, more natural. Even tho you can clearly see the imperfect version was rendered it still reminds me of myself using this product on a daily basis. Aesop is faking the evidence here, and it still works.

### Leica

Another example are [auctions](https://www.leitz-auction.com/en/Leica-M-Cameras-Ian-Berry/AI-100-Jahre-30160), of course. There are rare occasions where both things match up: The product looking _used_, and the perceived value through the object's history. It simply can't be true that an object has made memories of a lifetime and looks like new, thus the used look unconsciously adds supporting evidence in the story told about the object, no matter if true or not.

### Baseball gloves

I chose baseball gloves as a representation for all things sport related. It doesn't necessarily need to look like value to others, the satisfaction when looking at used sportswear mostly only applies to one person - the person that actually was the user. If you play a sport for multiple years, it being part of your life, making memories, the tools used while executing it undeniably hold a special value to its owner. This also applies to footballs, t-shirts, even shoes or other things.

## Anti-examples

There are also examples, and this perhaps makes up most of products we have in our world. I specifically want to take the time calling them out, because I think these are states of products you don't want to reach.

### Dirty shoes

One of the biggest anti-signals out there are probably dirty sneakers. There's a level of dirtness of sneakers which I personally find acceptable, I'd say it's 10-15% on the dirtness scale. After that, I throw my shoes into the washing machine. I see worker boots as general exception if you're permanently working in an environment, but then these shoes are made to get dirty. Sneakers and many other kinds of shoes are not. I would even go as far as saying that shoes are one of the most important pieces of clothing one can wear.

## The why

Looking back at all examples, I believe it comes down to three things. Wear stores memory - each scratch maps to a moment of one's life. Wear signals truth - a used object proves it was worth using, which is why even the faked version still works on us. And wear personalizes - two identical products leave the factory, but no two of them age the same way. Mass production ends at the moment of purchase.

## Software

Software is an interesting one. It can't scratch or fade, so it seems like it doesn't belong in this article. It does. The same principles decide what we perceive as valuable in software.

Think of software you're seeing ads from. The new iOS update, computer games, feature changelogs from your favorite social media platform. What do you see? Most of the time you will see inputs being filled with actual data. Not empty, unused screens. No perfect alignments, no button previews that look like someone just opened a preview of a digital design system - no - you will see actual content that reminds you of yourself, how you could use this feature. Apple never shows an empty Messages app, and Linear's landing page is full of half-done projects and open issues. The marketing version of software is always the used version.

Some products go one step further and turn the accumulated usage itself into the product. The GitHub contribution graph is a wear map - the digital version of the brassing on the Leica, proof of thousands of small touches, and developers show it off exactly like that. Spotify Wrapped does the same once a year, it takes the patina your listening left behind and hands it back to you as something to share. Duolingo streaks, Strava heatmaps - they all work because accumulated use is the most personal thing software can show you. I do a version of this on my own website: my [runs page](/writing/runs) is a live feed of every run I track. It started empty and gets better with every run - a page that collects patina.
