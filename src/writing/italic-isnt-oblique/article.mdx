# Italic isn't oblique

Italic and oblique get used as if they mean the same thing. They don't. They describe two different ways to make a slanted letter, and the difference shows up in two practical places: the styles you license, and the CSS you write to use them.

Wagram, the typeface this site uses, is a clear example. Its slanted styles are a true italic. The letters are redrawn, not just tipped over, and the name on each style says so.

## Two ways to lean

A slanted letter can be made two ways.

A _true italic_ is drawn from scratch. It isn't the upright letters tilted over, it's a separate set of shapes with roots in cursive handwriting. A lowercase _a_ shows it clearly. Upright it's usually two-story, with a hat on top. In a true italic it often becomes the single-story shape you'd write by hand. The _f_ grows a tail, the _e_ tightens. The letters don't just lean, they change.

An _oblique_ is the upright letters, slanted. Same shapes, pushed over a few degrees. Nothing is redrawn, though a well-made oblique adds small optical corrections rather than shearing the roman mechanically.

![A true italic redraws the letters. An oblique just slants the upright ones.](/images/writing/italic-vs-oblique.svg)

The split usually follows the category. Most sans-serifs ship obliques. Helvetica's "italic" is one. Serifs almost always draw a true italic instead, and Wagram follows that convention.

## A serif that draws a true italic

Wagram is a serif, so by tradition it carries a cursive italic, and it does. Slant the emphasis on this page and the letters don't merely lean: the _a_ drops to a single story, the _f_ grows a tail, the roman gives way to a flowing, handwritten voice. The shapes change, not just their angle.

The name on each style states the choice plainly. Italic, not Oblique.

## The browser only knows one slot

In CSS, the distinction mostly disappears.

There are two style values, `font-style: italic` and `font-style: oblique`, but font matching works in slots. Registering a face means mapping a file to a slot:

```css
@font-face {
  font-family: "Wagram";
  font-style: italic; /* the slot, not a claim about the design */
  src: url(/fonts/wagram/WagramNonCommercial-Italic.otf) format("opentype");
}
```

A true italic and an oblique both file under `italic` here; the slot is the same either way. From there, `font-style: italic` resolves to it, and so do `<em>` and `<i>`, since they default to italic. The browser draws no distinction, and neither does the reader.

Without a slanted face, the browser makes one. It shears the roman by roughly 14 degrees, the synthetic version of a naive oblique and a step below a face drawn for the purpose. That slant can also be requested directly with `font-style: oblique 14deg`.

So the terminology matters to the people who draw and license type. To the browser, there is only upright and not.
